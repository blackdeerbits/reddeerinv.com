Research Note · June 1, 2026

Running Local Models at Home

The headline: On an 8GB Mac Mini, no single small model is good at everything, but two of them are genuinely useful on their respective strengths. The practical takeaway is a split toolkit — not a single default — and the discovery that prompt format matters more than model choice for agentic tasks.

The setup: LM Studio on an M2 Mac Mini with 8GB of unified memory. No cloud APIs, no subscriptions, no data leaving the machine. I tested six models — five local through LM Studio, one cloud-only — across 30 prompts at three temperatures with three repeats each, using a scoring rubric that penalizes hallucination and broken tool calls harder than formatting mistakes.

This note is what I learned. It is not a recommendation, not a benchmark report, and not a comparison you should use to buy hardware. It is a record of what these models actually do when you sit down and use them for real tasks.

1. Why I Did This

The local inference space is moving fast. Six months ago, running an 8B model on consumer hardware meant watching a slideshow of barely-coherent text. Today, a $600 computer can serve useful output from several different architectures. But "useful" is not uniform — different models hallucinate in different ways, handle tools differently, and break on different kinds of prompts.

I wanted to understand the actual capability surface of these small open models without a vendor's marketing layer between me and what they produce. The only way to do that is to run them yourself, on your own hardware, with your own prompts.

2. The Models

The six models tested, with their actual architectures:

Model Architecture Active Params Total Size Source
Gemma 4 E2B (Google) Dense (E2B = 2B effective) 2B ~2B Local (LM Studio)
LFM 2.5-8B-A1B (Liquid AI) Mixture of Attention (MQA) ~2.5B 8B Local (LM Studio)
Ternary-Bonsai-8B 2-bit MLX quantized ~8B (degraded) 8B (2-bit) Local (LM Studio)
Qwen 3.5 4B (Alibaba) Dense 4B 4B Local (LM Studio)
Qwopus 3.5 9B Dense 9B 9B Local (LM Studio)
ZAYA1-8B (Zyphra) Dense + CCA attention 8B 8B Cloud API only

Note on "8B": The class labels are misleading. Gemma 4 E2B is a 2B-parameter model with a clean training recipe. LFM 2.5 has 8B total but only ~2.5B active per forward pass. Ternary-Bonsai is 8B quantized to 2 bits — fast, but lossy. Qwen 3.5 is genuinely 4B. Qwopus is 9B. Only ZAYA1-8B is a true 8B dense model, and that one runs in the cloud.

Calling these "five 8B models" is wrong. They are five different architectures at five different sizes, and trying to fit them into the same bucket obscures more than it reveals.

3. How I Tested

Thirty prompts across seven categories, each reflecting a task I actually perform — HOA board emails, code debugging, investment analysis, tool orchestration, technical writing. Each prompt has a rubric with pass/partial/fail criteria and a cost-of-failure weight (HIGH = 3, MED = 2, LOW = 1). A hallucinated financial planning recommendation weighs 3x more than a formatting issue in a summary.

Every model ran at three temperatures (0.3, 0.7, 1.0) with three repeats per setting. Standard benchmarking practice — a model that passes at 0.7 might fail at 0.3 simply because it got conservative with formatting. All local models used LM Studio with 8K max tokens and a 3-second pause between prompts to avoid running out of memory on the 8GB machine.

ZAYA1-8B was run through the Zyphra cloud API. Its daily playground quota (100K tokens) expired before the full suite could finish, so its numbers reflect only the agentic prompts that completed — not a clean head-to-head against the local models.

4. What I Found

Gemma 4 E2B — The All-Rounder

Gemma 4 E2B at temperature 0.7 was the strongest overall performer. It completed all 90 runs without error. Its outputs were the most concise and the most accurate on code, factual writing, and summarization. When it could not answer a question, it said so cleanly — no hallucinated service names, no fabricated entities, no "let me make something up that sounds plausible."

The weighted score of 72.1% at 0.7 is the best single-model result in this test. However, the caveat is that Gemma's token efficiency is partly an illusion. Google's OpenAI-compatible API reports `completion_tokens` that exclude content inside `` tags. On a test prompt asking for a one-word answer, the API reported 2 completion tokens; the real total was approximately 100. For anyone modeling economics or context window usage, approximately 80–90% of Gemma's compute work is hidden from the reported metrics.

LFM 2.5 — The Tool-Caller

LFM 2.5 was the only model that reliably built multi-step tool chains — three sequential calls in the right order, every time. This mattered specifically on agentic prompts where the task required gathering data from multiple sources before producing an answer. At temperature 1.0, LFM scored 100% on tool-calling prompts.

The tradeoff was token volume. LFM emits its reasoning chain explicitly in the API response, and that reasoning was frequently 50–70% of total output tokens. Whether it paid off was uneven: sometimes the reasoning improved the answer, sometimes it was noise. On planning prompts, LFM fabricated a fake email service called "Zervos" with invented URLs and DNS records — a hallucination that Gemma avoided entirely.

Ternary-Bonsai 8B and Qwen 3.5 4B — The Middle

Both completed most runs but were inconsistent on edge cases. Bonsai's 2-bit quantization made it the fastest model on disk, but the precision loss occasionally caused it to misinterpret instructions or skip subtasks. Qwen was balanced — it never failed spectacularly, but it leaned toward brevity on long prompts, producing shallow analysis when the task demanded depth.

Neither model is bad. Neither model is the best at anything specific. They are usable defaults for straightforward tasks where you don't need tool calling or deep analysis.

Qwopus 3.5 9B — The Disqualified

Qwopus did not finish reliably. Roughly half the runs crashed with server-side errors from LM Studio. On the runs that did complete, quality was middle of the pack — comparable to Qwen, not better despite the larger parameter count. Instability at this level is disqualifying for daily use. If it cannot finish the run, it does not matter how good its best output is.

ZAYA1-8B — The Incomplete Signal

ZAYA1-8B completed its agentic prompts without error, producing clean tool calls in OpenAI format. Its output quality was comparable to LFM on those specific prompts. However, the full evaluation could not complete due to the cloud playground's 100K daily token limit.

The Zaya evaluation I published separately covers it in more depth (full disclosure: the termination governor problem makes it impractical for agentic workflows), but the headline here is that ZAYA's cloud-only availability and quota constraints make it a non-option for daily local use regardless of quality.

5. The Discoveries I Did Not Expect

Two patterns emerged during this test that were not part of the original question.

Format Sensitivity

Early runs used simplified tool definitions. Both LFM and Gemma reverted to their native tool-calling syntax instead of valid JSON, and both scored near zero on tool prompts. After switching the prompt format to the OpenAI JSON schema, scores jumped materially — from 14% to 64% on agentic prompts.

The model behavior here is shaped by what the authors used during post-training, not by raw capability. An LFM that scores 14% in one format and 100% in another is not "learning" — it is matching the surface pattern it was trained on. This means prompt format is not a minor variable. It is the dominant variable for tool-calling tasks, and it does not show up in standard benchmarks that assume a fixed format.

Reasoning Overhead

LFM exposes its reasoning chain explicitly. Gemma's reasoning appears to happen inside weights rather than visible text. LFM's visible reasoning was frequently half or more of total output tokens. That is expensive on an 8GB machine running locally, and whether it paid off was uneven: sometimes the thinking improved the answer, sometimes it was noise.

Gemma's leaner outputs were often better on the same dimensions, which raises an uncomfortable question: how much of LFM's reasoning is genuine improvement versus learned deliberation behavior that burns tokens without adding accuracy?

Google's Token Accounting Trap

Gemma 4's reported `completion_tokens` from the AI Studio API excludes hidden `` content. This is not a minor discrepancy — on hard prompts, the real token count is approximately 8x higher than reported. ZAYA and Nemotron (tested separately) report reasoning tokens transparently. Anyone comparing costs across models needs to account for this, and most benchmark comparisons do not.

6. The Practical Outcome

I started this expecting one model to dominate. Instead I ended up with a split toolkit. Gemma for writing and code drafts. LFM when I need a reliable tool-calling sequence. Neither one is close to closed-source cloud models on reasoning or factual recall, but both are genuinely useful on their respective strengths.

The practical consequence is that I keep two models loaded in LM Studio and rotate them depending on the task. That is messy compared to a single default, but the outputs are meaningfully better than forcing one model to do everything.

For anyone building a local AI stack — whether for privacy, cost control, or experimentation — the honest takeaway is that local inference is now good enough to be annoying in useful ways. You get real capability, but you also get real friction: format sensitivity, uneven behavior, the need to switch models per task. Deciding how to spend that friction is the actual question.

Models will get better. Hardware will change. My prompts probably need calibration. But the split-toolkit finding is structural, not temporary — because the architectures are diverging, not converging. Small dense models optimized for writing, large MoE models for reasoning, quantized models for speed. The local inference stack will look less like a single default and more like a toolbox, and that is fine.

Appendix: Methodology & Data Sources

Hardware: Apple Mac Mini M2, 8GB unified memory, macOS, LM Studio.

Scoring: Weighted rubric with HIGH (3), MED (2), LOW (1) failure costs. Maximum weighted score 136. Results reported at temperature 0.7 unless noted.

Limitations:

  • All models run on a single 8GB machine — results may differ on higher-end hardware with more memory bandwidth.
  • Temperature 0.7 was used as the primary comparison point, but LFM performed best at 1.0 on tool-calling.
  • ZAYA results are incomplete due to cloud API quota limits. Full ZAYA evaluation is published separately.
  • Gemma 4 token counts exclude hidden thought content — see Zaya evaluation note for details.
  • Models tested in late May 2026. Weights may have been updated since.

Raw data: Full outputs, prompts, and scoring scripts are available on request.
